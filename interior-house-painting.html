What a room repaint actually involves

Rolling paint on a wall is maybe a third of the job. The part that decides whether your walls still look good in five years happens before the finish coat goes on, and it's the first thing that gets skipped by anyone racing to the next house.

1. Protect the room

Furniture comes to the middle and gets covered. Floors get covered wall to wall — not a drop cloth thrown under the ladder. Outlet covers and switch plates come off and go in a labelled bag, because taping around them leaves a ridge you'll notice forever.

2. Fix the wall

Nail holes, anchor holes, dents from a headboard, popped screws, hairline settling cracks — filled, then sanded flush with the wall. If a patch is still proud of the surface it will catch light and show through paint no matter how many coats go over it.

3. Prime what needs it

Not every wall needs a full prime coat, and I'm not going to charge you for one you don't need. Patches do. Water stains do. Glossy or previously oil-painted surfaces do. Going from a deep color to a light one usually does. I'll tell you which of these applies to your room at the estimate, not halfway through.

4. Cut in by hand, then roll

Every edge — ceiling line, corners, around trim, around outlets — gets cut in with a brush. A steady hand gives a crisper line than tape, and tape has a habit of pulling paint off older trim when it comes up.

5. Two coats, then look at it in real light

Two coats as standard. Then I walk the room with a light at a low angle looking for holidays, roller lap marks and thin spots, and I fix them before I tell you it's done.

Painting before you move in is the cheapest time to do it

An empty house has no furniture to move, nothing to protect, and no schedule to work around. If you've just bought a place in Plymouth or the west metro and you're closing in a few weeks, that gap is the single best window you'll ever get for painting — and it costs less than the same work does once your couch is in the room.

Picking colors without regretting it

Color reads completely differently on a wall than it does on a chip under store lighting. Two things save people a lot of grief:

  • Buy a sample pot and paint a real two-foot square on the actual wall. Then look at it in morning light, afternoon light, and with the lamps on at night. North-facing rooms in Minnesota go cold and grey in winter, and a color that looked warm in the store can turn on you.
  • Look at it next to your trim and your floor, not next to the other chips. Chips lie because they're surrounded by white card.

I'm not a designer and I won't pretend to be one, but I've seen a lot of colors go on a lot of walls. If you ask me whether something is going to fight your floors, I'll tell you what I actually think.

How long it takes

A single bedroom, walls only, is usually one day. Add trim and it's typically two. A main floor is normally three to five days depending on patching and how many colors are involved. You'll get a realistic day count in your written estimate — and if something changes, you hear about it from me before it becomes a problem.

How many coats will my walls need?

Two, on almost every job. Going from a dark color to a light one, or covering a strong red or deep blue, may need a primer coat first — I will tell you that at the estimate rather than discovering it halfway through and asking for more money.

What sheen should I use on walls?

For most Twin Cities homes: eggshell or matte in bedrooms and living rooms, and satin in kitchens, bathrooms, mudrooms, and hallways where walls get wiped down. Flat hides wall imperfections best but does not clean well. I will put the right sheen on your paint list.

Can you paint one accent wall?

Yes, and it is one of the cheapest ways to change how a room feels. It is a small job, so it often makes sense to pair it with another room you were already thinking about.
