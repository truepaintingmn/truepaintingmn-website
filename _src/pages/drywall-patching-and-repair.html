This is part of painting, not a separate upsell

Every house that has been lived in has a wall full of small damage. Picture hooks and anchor holes. A dent where a chair back hit the wall. A door handle that punched through behind a doorstop that fell off years ago. Screws that popped as the house settled through a few Minnesota winters.

Normal patching like this is included in an interior repaint. I'm not going to hand you a separate line item for filling the holes your pictures were hanging on.

What I patch

  • Nail holes, picture hook holes and drywall anchor holes
  • Dents and dings from furniture, doors and daily life
  • Popped drywall screws and nail pops
  • Hairline settling cracks, especially above door frames
  • Small holes up to roughly the size of your hand
  • Corner bead dings on outside corners
  • Old tape seams that have started to lift

How a patch is actually done

The difference between an invisible patch and one you can find from across the room is entirely in the sanding and the priming.

  • Fill, then let it dry properly. Compound shrinks as it dries. A deep hole needs a second pass after the first one sinks, or you get a dimple.
  • Feather it wide. A patch that's built up in a tight blob will always catch light. It gets spread out well past the damage and tapered to nothing.
  • Sand flush with the wall. Not "close to flush." Run your hand over it with your eyes shut — if you can feel it, you'll see it under paint.
  • Prime the patch. Bare joint compound is far more absorbent than the painted wall around it. Skip the primer and the patch drinks the paint, dries a different sheen, and shows as a dull spot forever. This is the step that gets skipped most often, and it's why you can spot other people's patches.

The honest limit

On a smooth wall, a properly done patch is genuinely invisible once painted. On a heavily textured wall, a good patch blends closely but texture is never a perfect match — anyone who promises otherwise is overselling. I'll tell you which one you have before I start, not after you've paid.

What I won't take on

Painting over a real problem doesn't fix it, and I'm not going to be the person who covers it up so it becomes the next person's discovery.

  • Large holes and full sheet replacement — you want a drywall contractor
  • Water-damaged drywall — the source has to be fixed and the area dried first, and damaged board usually has to come out
  • Anything with mold behind it — that's a remediation contractor, full stop
  • Ceiling replacement or large ceiling repairs
  • Texture matching across a whole ceiling or wall
  • Anything structural, electrical or plumbing-related

If you show me one of these I'll tell you straight, and I'm happy to come back and paint once the right trade has fixed it.
