Mid-century houses paint differently

Golden Valley filled in mostly through the 1940s, 50s and 60s, and it kept that character — ramblers and split-levels on established lots with mature trees, close enough to Minneapolis to walk into Theodore Wirth Park. The rooms are generally smaller and better proportioned than what got built thirty years later, and the trim profiles are simpler.

What's behind the paint is the part that matters. Houses of this age frequently have:

  • Plaster walls instead of drywall. Plaster is harder, more brittle, and cracks in long thin lines rather than crumbling. It needs a different patching approach and a bit more patience. A plaster crack that's just filled and painted will usually come back — it wants to be properly bridged.
  • Old oil-based paint on woodwork. Very common on trim and doors from this era. Modern latex will not bond to cured oil paint without a bonding primer first. Skip that and it peels in strips.
  • Many layers of previous paint. Six decades of repaints build up. On detailed trim the profile starts to soften and disappear.
  • Coved ceilings and rounded corners in some of the older stock, which are slower to cut in cleanly and worth doing carefully because they're a nice feature.

Before you book anyone: check the build year

A large share of Golden Valley's housing predates 1978, which means there may be lead-based paint under the layers. Federal rules put specific requirements on any contractor being paid to disturb paint in a home of that age, and they exist for a good reason — small children and lead dust are a serious combination.

Tell me the year your house was built when you call. I'll give you a straight answer about whether I can take the job, and if the answer is no, I'd rather say so than take your money. I'd give you the same advice about any painter you're considering: ask them the question and see whether they know what you're talking about.

Making smaller rooms feel bigger

Mid-century rooms are cozier than modern open plans, and paint is the cheapest lever you have on how a room feels. A few things that reliably work in these houses:

  • Paint the trim the same white as the ceiling. Continuous white at the edges makes the walls read as taller.
  • Skip the dark accent wall in a small room. It usually makes the room feel like a smaller box, not a bigger one.
  • Watch north-facing rooms. Minnesota winter light through a north window is cold and grey. Cool greys that look sophisticated in a showroom can go genuinely gloomy from November through March. Warm whites and soft off-whites hold up much better here.
